Where the week goes

The jobs that keep coming round in a practice.

Most of these started as a sensible habit when the firm was a couple of partners who knew every client by name, and they tend to stop working somewhere around the point the firm doubles.

Onboarding that's never the same twice

Two accountants bring on the same kind of client in two different ways, so the welcome email, the document list and the timing all depend on who happened to pick it up.

The document chase

Requests go out ad hoc, the reminders are manual (and sometimes doubled up), and the first advisory meeting gets spent asking for the paperwork instead of talking about the business.

A tracker each

Everyone keeps their own checklist or spreadsheet, so there's no one place that shows where a job's up to, and the knowledge lives in people's inboxes.

Partners flying blind

A client can go quiet for a fortnight and nobody notices until they chase you, because there's no easy way to see what's stalled and who owns it.

Writing the same email again

The engagement summary, the "here's what we need from you" note and the end of job update, written from scratch or from whatever was sent last time.

What comes in the door

Client questions, ATO correspondence and portal notifications all land in the same inboxes, and somebody has to sort out what's urgent and whose it is.

Most practices I talk to are already well set up: Xero Practice Manager or MYOB, FYI or a document portal, Microsoft 365 and a CRM of some kind. The gap is usually a standard way of working on top of the tools, not more tools, and the audit doesn't ask you to change any of them.

The line that doesn't move: AI in a practice drafts, chases and organises, and a registered agent decides. It never takes a tax position, never signs anything off and never lodges, identity checks stay with your people and your existing controls, and nothing it drafts goes to a client without someone reading it first.

An 18-person accounting firm

Three partners, three service lines (business advisory, tax compliance and bookkeeping), and a firm that's doubled in size in three years, with the systems already in place and the process still in people's heads.

What the interviews found

  • AcquisitionQuality riskA new client's first fortnight depends on who picked them up. Different welcome emails, different document lists and different timing, in a firm that sells itself on the relationship.
  • AcquisitionTime sinkEngagement summaries are written from scratch. Or from the last one sent, which is how last year's scope ends up in this year's letter.
  • DeliveryTime sinkDocuments are chased by hand. Ad hoc requests and manual reminders, so the right documents often aren't in before the first meeting.
  • DeliveryTime sinkEvery accountant keeps a tracker. Separate checklists and spreadsheets, and a status meeting each week to stitch them together.
  • DeliveryQuality riskStalled jobs go unnoticed. Nobody can see which onboardings have gone quiet or whose they are until the client rings.
  • SupportTime sinkIncoming mail is sorted by whoever's free. Client questions, ATO letters and portal notifications all in the same inboxes.

Where each one sits

Quick winsOne document request list per service line, with requests and reminders drafted for a person to check and send, and the same welcome email for everyone, written once.
Big swingsOne shared onboarding tracker that partners can see, built on the practice management system the firm already has.
Nice-to-havesSorting incoming mail into what's urgent and whose it is, and drafting the engagement summary from the proposal.
DeprioritiseAnything that's a tax position, an identity check or a sign-off. Those stay with a registered agent, so the audit leaves them out of the sums.

The plan

  1. First month: the document request lists and the welcome email, with every message drafted for an accountant to review and send.
  2. Months two and three: the shared tracker, piloted on five real onboardings across the three service lines before anybody's asked to change how they work.
  3. After that: sorting what comes in, once the firm's seen the first two working and trusts them.

Questions accountants ask

Before you book anything.

Will AI be preparing returns or giving tax advice?

No, and the audit won't recommend anything that does. AI can draft the request, chase the documents and organise the file, and a registered agent does the work that needs one and signs it off.

Does it work with Xero Practice Manager, MYOB or FYI?

The audit doesn't change your systems at all. It looks at what your team does around them, and any recommendation has to work with the practice management and document tools you're already using.

What about client confidentiality?

Anything I recommend has to keep client information inside tools that are approved for it, and the audit says plainly where a job shouldn't go anywhere near an AI tool at all.

What's in the report?

Where the week goes, written up from the interviews, then each workflow that's worth changing with a figure on it from your team's own numbers, the ones AI should stay away from and why, and a plan for the next few months ranked by effort and by what each change is worth.

Do I have to hire you to build anything?

No. The report's yours, and it's written so that you or anyone else can run with it. If you do bring me back for a build inside 60 days, the audit fee comes off it.

Do we need to be using AI already?

No. Plenty of the firms I talk to are using it in bits and pieces or not at all, and that's fine, because the audit starts from where your week goes and not from the tools.

How long does it take?

That mostly depends on your diary and how many of the team I'm talking to, so I'll give you dates on the call rather than guess here and get it wrong.

What happens to what we tell you?

The interviews are recorded so that nothing gets lost, the recordings and notes are only used for your audit, and nothing about your business gets published without your written OK, the case study included.
