Where the week goes

Some of the jobs that keep coming round in a not-for-profit.

Nobody joins a not-for-profit to fill in funder templates, and yet more often than not that's where a good part of the week goes, mostly in the evenings before a deadline.

Grant applications

The same program described again for every funder's form and word limit, usually by whoever has a spare evening, and last year's best version is sitting in somebody's inbox.

Acquittals and funder reports

Figures pulled from the program records, the accounts and the case files, then written up in a different format for each funder, and a lot of them fall due at the same time.

Board papers

The CEO's report, the finance summary and the program updates, put together by hand for every meeting, mostly from things that have already been written somewhere else.

Keeping the CRM up to date

Donor, member and volunteer details copied in from emails, forms and spreadsheets, which is double handling and usually where the records start to drift apart.

Volunteers

Rosters, reminders, inductions and the last-minute swaps, sorted out by email and phone one person at a time, and often by the one coordinator who knows how it all fits together.

Thanking donors

The thank-you that should go out within a few days and the receipt that goes with it, which matter a great deal to the person who gave and tend to be the first thing to slip when the team's stretched.

Most smaller not-for-profits are running Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, a donor or member CRM (or a spreadsheet that's grown into one), and Xero or MYOB. The audit works on top of what you've already got, and doesn't ask you to change any of it.

The line that doesn't move: AI in a not-for-profit drafts the applications, the reports and the board papers, and leaves every decision about the people you support with your staff. It never decides who's eligible or who gets a service, nothing goes to a funder or the board until a person has checked it against the real figures, and client information stays inside tools that are approved for it.

A 15-person community services not-for-profit

Illustrative audit · fictional business · figures modelled from stated assumptions

A CEO, a finance and operations manager, ten staff across three funded programs, two in admin and fundraising and about forty volunteers, on Microsoft 365, a donor CRM that only one person really knows how to use, and Xero.

What the interviews found

  • AcquisitionTime sinkEvery grant application starts from a blank page. The programs have been described dozens of times, but each funder asks in a different shape and nobody can find the best version from last year.
  • AcquisitionQuality riskApplications quote figures that don't match the last report. The numbers depend on who wrote it, and a funder reading both can see the gap.
  • DeliveryTime sinkAcquittals are built by hand from three places. Program records, Xero and the case files, pulled together at the end of every reporting period.
  • DeliveryTime sinkBoard papers take most of a week. The CEO's report and the program updates are rewritten from emails and reports that already exist.
  • DeliveryQuality riskThe stories funders ask for are found in a rush. Good examples from the year get lost because nobody wrote them down at the time.
  • SupportTime sinkVolunteer rosters run on email and phone calls. Swaps, reminders and inductions all go through one coordinator.
  • SupportQuality riskThe donor CRM lives in one person's head. When they're on leave, the thank-yous wait and the records drift.

Where each one sits

Quick winsBoard papers and donor thank-yous, drafted from what's already been written, for a person to check and send.
Big swingsGrant applications and acquittals drafted from one approved description of each program and the real figures, with every number checked by a person before it goes to a funder.
Nice-to-havesVolunteer rosters and reminders, and a simple way of writing the stories down as they happen, with the client's OK.
DeprioritiseAnything that decides who's eligible, who gets a service or what a client needs. That stays with your staff, so the audit leaves it out of the sums.

The plan

  1. First month: board papers, because they're written for people who already know the work and nothing in them is about a client, which makes them the easiest place to show the team it works.
  2. Months two and three: grant applications and acquittals, starting with one program and one funder before anybody's asked to change the rest.
  3. After that: volunteers and the CRM, once the team's seen the first two working and trusts them.

Questions not-for-profits ask

Before you book anything.

Is there a not-for-profit price?

Yes. Not-for-profits get 20% off a standard audit, so it starts at $1,600 + GST, and while the founding audits last you can take one of those at $1,500 + GST instead. The two don't stack.

Will AI be deciding who gets support?

No, and the audit won't recommend anything that does. AI can draft the application, the report and the board paper, and your staff decide who's eligible and what a client needs, every time.

What about our clients' information?

Anything I recommend has to keep client information inside tools that are approved for it, with nothing pasted into a personal or free AI account, and the audit says plainly where a job shouldn't go anywhere near an AI tool at all, which in a lot of services means the case notes.

Can AI write our grant applications?

It can draft them from one approved description of each program and your real figures, and that's where most of the time goes. A person still reads every one before it goes in, because a funder is backing your organisation, and an application that promises something you can't deliver costs far more than the hours it saved.

Our board will want to know about the risks.

That's fair, and the report's written so that you can put it in front of them. Every recommendation says what could go wrong and who checks the work, and the list of jobs AI should stay away from is in there too.

What's in the report?

Where the week goes, written up from the interviews, then each workflow that's worth changing with a figure on it from your team's own numbers, the ones AI should stay away from and why, and a plan for the next few months ranked by effort and by what each change is worth.

Do I have to hire you to build anything?

No. The report's yours, and it's written so that you or anyone else can run with it. If you do bring me back for a build inside 60 days, the audit fee comes off it.

Do we need to be using AI already?

No. Plenty of the firms I talk to are using it in bits and pieces or not at all, and that's fine, because the audit starts from where your week goes and not from the tools.

How long does it take?

That mostly depends on your diary and how many of the team I'm talking to, so I'll give you dates on the call rather than guess here and get it wrong.

What happens to what we tell you?

The interviews are only recorded with your OK, so that nothing gets lost, and the recordings, transcripts and notes are kept where only I can get at them and used for your audit and nothing else. Client details stay in business-grade tools that don't train on what goes into them, names come out wherever they don't need to be in, and a person checks anything that matters before it's used. The recordings are deleted 90 days after the debrief, or sooner if you ask, and nothing about your business gets published without your written OK, the case study included.
